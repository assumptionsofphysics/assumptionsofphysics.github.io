Counterexamples. This week I was working on the Schroedinger equation, trying to understand what sets of necessary and sufficient conditions are equivalent to it. One necessary condition is that the evolution of a mixture is the mixture of the evolutions. Is this a base condition (i.e. true in all physical theories) or not? It turns out it isn't, because there are counterexamples. Let's see how all of this works.

In Reverse Physics, we analyze mathematical structures by finding equivalent physical conditions. In this case, one may wonder why Schroedinger's equation is linear. In a [previous essay](https://assumptionsofphysics.org/essays/2026-04-15-why-superpositions) we saw how the linearity in the vector space corresponds exactly with the linearity of density matrices. The first describes superpositions, which are just mathematical relationships with no direct physical meaning, while the second describes statistical mixing which does represent something physical: the ability to take one preparation $$p$$ percent of the time and the other $$1-p$$ percent of the time. The linearity of the Schroedinger equation, then, is a statement about statistical mixtures: the evolution of a statistical mixture is equal to the statistical mixture of the evolution. That is, if $$\rho_1$$ and $$\rho_2$$ are initial states, and $$\Phi_t$$ is the time evolution map, then $$\Phi_t(p \rho_1 + (1-p) \rho_2) = p \Phi_t(\rho_1) + (1-p) \Phi_t(\rho_2)$$. In fact, we can [prove](https://assumptionsofphysics.org/autogen/briefs/012-SchroedingerEvolution.pdf) that the Schroedinger equation is this condition plus a bijection on pure states: past and future states are mapped one-to-one; the dynamics is deterministic and reversible.

Now, the question is: is the preservation of statistical mixture for time evolution something that all physical theories must possess? Or is it something that applies only to special cases? This is important since, in the foundations of quantum mechanics, some people have been proposing non-linear time evolution as a way to extend the theory or to model measurement processes. Would this attempt make sense conceptually, or not?

Suppose that our statistical mixture really represents independent preparations at different times. That is, half the time I start with a particle prepared in one way and the other half with a particle prepared in a different way. Then, clearly, each individual evolution depends on its own independent initial condition, and cannot possibly depend on what happens in the past or in the future. In this case, preservation of statistical mixtures has to follow. But not all statistical ensembles represent this case.

There are two other ways statistical ensembles are used. They could be describing fractions of a system at the same time: the system consists $$p$$ percent of substance $$A$$ and $$1-p$$ percent of substance $$B$$. Or, for a thermodynamic equilibrium, they could be describing the fraction of time the system visits the state under the effect of system/environment fluctuations. In both these cases, a physical process can depend on the whole distribution.

For example, in chemistry, an [autocatalytic reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocatalysis) is a reaction in which one of the outputs is also a catalyst of the reaction. That is, it makes it go faster. For example, we could have a reaction $$A+B \mapsto 2B$$. The respective concentrations $$(p_a, p_b)$$ may change in time according to $$d_t p_a = -k p_a p_b$$ and $$d_t p_b = k p_a p_b$$, with $$p_a + p_b = 1$$ at all times. This is not a linear equation, so, while the total is conserved, statistical mixtures are not preserved. For example, $$(1,0)$$ and $$(0,1)$$ are both equilibria, because this reaction won't start if there isn't some $$B$$ around, and will stop if there is only $$B$$. However, $$(p,1-p)$$ will evolve toward $$(0,1)$$.

This counterexample showcases two things. First, that there is no single interpretation of probability, but you need to understand, physically, what is it that you are talking about. Second, that different physical processes can exist depending on what we are describing. We can now imagine, then, non-linear dynamics on the space of ensembles if those represent something like statistical fluctuations of an equilibrium instead of simply lack of knowledge. In foundations, then, it is not so much whether some mathematical property is true or not, but under what conditions and assumptions it remains valid.
