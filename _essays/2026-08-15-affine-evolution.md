Counterexamples. This week I was working on the Schroedinger equation, trying to understand what are sets of necessary and sufficient condition. One nevessary condition is that the evolution of a mixture is the mixture of the evolutions. Is this base condition (i.e. true in all physical theories) or not? It turns out it isn't, and I know because there are counterexamples. Let's see how all of this works.

In Reverse Physics, we analyze mathematical structures by finding equivalent physical conditions. In this case, one may wonder why Schroedinger's equation is linear. In a [previous essay](https://assumptionsofphysics.org/essays/2026-04-15-why-superpositions) we saw how the linearity in the vector space corresponds exactly with the linearity coming from statistical distribution. The first describes superposition, which are just mathematical relationship with no direct physical meaning, while the second describe statistical mixing which, does represent something physical: the ability to take one preparation $$p$$ per-cent of the times and and the other $$1-p$$ percent of the times. The linearity of the Schroedinger equation, then, is a statement about statistical mixtures: the evolution of a statistical mixture is equal to the statistical mixture of the evolution. That is, if $$\rho_1$$ and $$\rho_2$$ are initial states, and $$\Phi_t$$ is the time evolution map, then $$\Phi_t(p \rho_1 + (1-p) \rho_2) = p \Phi_t(\rho_1) + (1-p) \Phi_t(\rho_2)$$. In fact, we can [prove](https://assumptionsofphysics.org/autogen/briefs/012-SchroedingerEvolution.pdf) that the Schroedinger equation is this condition plus a bijection on pure states: past and future states are mapped one-to-one, the dynamics is deterministic and reversible.

Now, the question is: is the preservation of statistical mixture for time evolution something that all physical theories must posses? Or is it something that applies only to special cases? This is important since, in the foundation of quantum mechanics, some people have been proposing non-linear time evolution as a way to extend the theory or to model measurement processes. Would this attempt make sense conceptually, or not?

Suppose that our statistical mixture really represents independent preparations at different instances in times. That is, half the time I start with a particle prepared in one way and the other half with a particle prepared in a different way. Then, clearly, each individual evolution depends on its own independent initial condition, and cannot possibly depend on what is going to happen in the past or in the future. In this case, preservation of statistical mixture has to follow. But not all statistical ensembles represent this case.

There are other two ways statistical ensembles are used. They could be describing fractions of a system at the same time: the system consists $$p$$ percent of substance $$A$$ and $$1-p$$ percent of substance $$B$$. Or, for a thermodynamic equilibria, they could be describing the fraction of time the system visits the state under the effect of system/environment fluctuations. In both these cases, a physical process can depend on the whole distribution.

For example, in chemistry, an [autocatalytic reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocatalysis) is a reaction in which one of the output is also a catalyst of the reaction. That is, it makes it go faster. For example, we could have a reaction $$A+B \mapsto 2B$$, then the respecting concentrations $$(p_a, p_b)$$ may change in time according to $$d_t p_a = -k p_a p_b$$ and $$d_t = k p_a p_b$$, with $$p_a + p_b = 1$$ at all times. This is not a linear equation, so, while the total is conserved, statistical mixtures are not preserved. For example, both $$(1,0)$$ and $$(0,1)$$ are both equilibria, because this reaction won't start if there isn't some $$B$$ around, and will stop if there is only $$B$$. However, $$(p,1-p)$$ will evolve toward $$(0,1)$$.

This counterexample showcases two things. First, that there is no single interpretation of probability, but you need to understand, physically, what is it that you are talking about. Second, that the different physical process can exist depending on what we are describing. In foundations, then, it is not so much whether some mathematical property is true or not, but under what conditions and assumptions it remains valid.
