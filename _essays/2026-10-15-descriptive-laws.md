---
title: Physical laws are descriptive, not prescriptive
summary: Physical laws describe what happens under particular conditions, not what nature must do
category: Foundations of physics

We often say that nature "obeys" the laws of physics. A particle "must" follow its equation of motion. A process is "forbidden" by a conservation law. This language is convenient, and we generally understand what it means. But if we take it too literally, it suggests that the equations somehow tell nature what to do. However, this is not the case: physical laws are descriptive, not prescriptive. They describe what happens; they do not prescribe what should happen. This distinction is at the heart of a few misunderstandings in the foundations of physics.

Consider the measurement problem in quantum mechanics. We describe unitary evolution with the Schroedinger equation, while a measurement is described through probabilities and a change of state associated with the outcome. If these are instructions, we seem to have a problem: how does nature decide which one to follow? How does the particle know that it is being measured? The language suggests that nature needs a way to select which rule to execute.

But why should having two descriptions be a problem? A pendulum and an orbiting planet have different Hamiltonians, and therefore different equations of motion. Nobody asks how nature decides which Hamiltonian to use. They are different physical situations, and we describe them differently. The same attitude can be taken toward unitary evolution and measurement: we have different processes to describe. We need to characterize the conditions under which each description applies. The existence of two descriptions, by itself, is no contradiction.

Of course, if we additionally require that every process, including measurement, must be described by unitary evolution, we have a further problem to solve. But that requirement is an additional assumption. It does not follow simply from the success of unitary evolution in the situations where we use it. The descriptive perspective lets us separate the physical question of how to characterize measurement from the question of how nature chooses between instructions. The latter question comes from the picture we attached to the equations.

Another example is the principle of least action. We say that a particle "chooses" the path that minimizes the action. How can it compare paths it has not taken? Does it need to know its destination in advance? Even [Feynman's discussion](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_19.html) uses the image of a particle examining neighboring paths. It is memorable language, but it can make a mathematical relationship sound like a decision procedure. More precisely, the classical principle says that the action is stationary under small variations of the physical path with fixed endpoints. As we show in our paper on the [geometric and physical interpretation of the action principle](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-39145-y), stationary action is really just a different way to state that the path is everywhere tangent to the flow of states in extended phase space. The particle does not need to compare possible paths: the principle describes how its path follows the flow of states.

In philosophy of physics, we also encounter the idea that laws "produce" the future from the past, but this should not be taken literally. Consider, for example, a deterministic and reversible evolution, like unitary evolution in quantum mechanics. Given a state, the equations allow us to reconstruct the past as well as predict the future. We do not conclude that the present produces the past because we can calculate backward. Why should calculating forward, by itself, establish that the equations produce the future? The mathematical relationship tells us how states at different times are related. Giving that relationship the power to bring events into existence is a further philosophical commitment.

These examples share the same shift in language. A description becomes an instruction, and then we ask how that instruction is carried out. Nature needs to choose an equation, a particle needs to compare paths, or a law needs to manufacture the next instant. We have introduced an extra task and then become puzzled about how it is accomplished. Moreover, if the laws are prescriptive, they must be complete: nature must know how everything must evolve at all times. But if the laws are descriptive, and our description is unavoidably limited, then the laws are not complete. The incompleteness of quantum theory can be understood better in this context: quantum theory offers a better description precisely because it accounts for the incompleteness of our description. It may very well be that a quantum theory of gravity will not be more complete, as superdeterminists hope, but even less complete, because our descriptions are limited in different ways.
