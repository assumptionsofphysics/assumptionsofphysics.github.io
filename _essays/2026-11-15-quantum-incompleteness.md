title: Quantum incompleteness is a feature, not a bug
summary: A complete model can be applied to only one thing at one time in one way

Is quantum mechanics complete? [Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.47.777) argued that the wavefunction does not give a complete description of physical reality. [Bohr replied](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.48.696) that quantum mechanics meets all reasonable demands for completeness within its scope. Much of the modern debate follows those lines. But there is another question that I don't see brought up: do we actually want our descriptions to be complete?

To illustrate the problem in the usual terms, take an electron, which we regard as a fundamental particle. Suppose we prepare its spin up along the vertical axis. If we measure the spin along a horizontal axis, the theory gives equal probabilities for the two outcomes. We can specify the spin state exactly and still not know which result we will get for this particular electron. Where does this extra information come from? That is the incompleteness question. The naive answer is that we are missing something. If we had a more complete description of the electron, we would have the information to perfectly predict all outcomes of all possible measurements. The preparation is simply randomly preparing these extra variables in advance. However, this does not work: the type of probabilities quantum mechanics successfully predicts cannot be simply recovered in that way. Maybe this extra information is in the detector? Maybe it is part of a lower-level random process? At this point, we do not have a theory that is universally accepted to _complete_ quantum mechanics.

Some argue that the problem is in the word completeness itself. A theory should be regarded as complete only if it gives the best account of the system and processes being studied. Suppose we did have a working theory with extra variables for the electron or the detector that would tell us which outcome we’ll get. If we can’t get at them experimentally, in what sense have we improved the theory? Can we tell, before we measure the spin, when one outcome is more likely than the other? [Colbeck and Renner](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.5173) showed that no extension of quantum theory can improve its predictions under their assumptions. In this sense the quantum state may be complete: the extra information would not buy us a better prediction. Some will argue that those assumptions are not really required... and the debate goes on.

What I find puzzling about this debate is that, most of the time, we use the wavefunction in contexts where we *know* it doesn't give a complete description. We can describe protons, atoms and molecules, which are not fundamental systems. Even the states of macroscopic systems, like cavities, are described by wavefunctions. If we prepare two molecules in the same state, such that their wavefunctions are the same, are we saying we prepared all their atoms and all their quarks, gluons and electrons, in the same exact state? Most certainly not. But for the processes we are interested in, they are similar enough to count as the same state. When I bring this up, nobody seems to be bothered by this or see a problem. But there is a critical point: this leeway is actually what makes physics possible. Suppose that, to prepare two molecules in the same state, you had to prepare all atoms and all their quarks, gluons and electrons, in the same exact state. This would not just be insanely impossible in practice: since everything in the universe interacts with everything else, and things tend to move around in the universe, we would never be able to prepare the same state twice. A truly complete description applies to only one thing and only once. There would be no reproducibility. And with no ability to reproduce anything, there is no physics, there is no science.

The reason we can do physics at all is that we can find systems and processes that can be studied independently, to the precision we need, of both the internal structure and the interaction with the surroundings. We will never prepare two atoms in exactly the same circumstances, or control every interaction between an atom and our detector. Yet we can repeat the experiment precisely because its result, or the distribution of its results, is insensitive to those differences. The same has to be true when we prepare and measure an electron. Calling it fundamental does not make our preparations or measurements perfect. What matters is that we can identify what has to be the same from one instance to the next and leave the rest out. That is why incompleteness is a feature, not a bug.
